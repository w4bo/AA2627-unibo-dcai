The interpretability issue

A validation score tells us how often a model predicts well, but leaves its reasoning hidden.

  • A predictor can use information recorded after the outcome.
  • Different fitted models can reach similar scores using different relationships.
  • Understanding depends on the person and task, not just the algorithm.

Question: would you deploy a bike-demand model that uses registered rentals to predict total rentals?

Theory 1, 0–3 min. Ask students to identify the availability problem. Source: https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/interpretability.html

Prediction, explanation, and causality

Claim What evidence supports it?
The model predicts well on these later hours Held-out performance
The model uses temperature in this prediction A rule, coefficient, or attribution
Raising temperature would increase real demand A causal study with appropriate assumptions

These are different claims. An explanation describes a fitted predictor; it does not automatically explain the data-generating process.

Logistic regression

For high demand, \(p(x)=P(y=1\mid x)\):

\[\log\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)}=\beta_0+\sum_j\beta_j x_j\]

  • Each coefficient changes log-odds, holding other inputs fixed.
  • \(\exp(\beta_j)\) is an odds multiplier, not a probability change.
  • Here, continuous inputs are standardized; hour is one-hot encoded with hour 0 as reference.
  • Interactions require additional features. More terms can reduce readability.

17–19 min. https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/logistic.html . A continuous coefficient refers to one training standard deviation; category coefficients compare with the omitted category.

Read the fitted logistic coefficients

The plot shows the eight largest absolute coefficients, not the whole model.

  • hour_17 compares hour 17 with hour 0.
  • temperature_C refers to one training standard deviation, holding other transformed features fixed.
  • Magnitude depends on encoding and scaling. It is not a universal importance score.

Question: can a positive coefficient imply a fixed increase of 20 percentage points?

19–21 min. No: the sigmoid makes probability changes depend on the starting log-odds. Include the preprocessing when interpreting a model.

Log-odds and probability

Hold hour and weather conditions fixed, then vary temperature in the fitted logistic model.

The log-odds response is linear in standardized temperature. The sigmoid turns it into a curved probability response; the same temperature change need not give the same probability change.

Theory 1: use this with the coefficient discussion, within 17–21 min. These are controlled model slices, not observations or causal effects. Working day = 1, adverse weather = 0, other inputs at training medians. Inspect sparse combinations cautiously. The fitted coefficients, not invented parameters, generate both curves.

Decision tree: rules and paths

  • Each split asks a threshold question; a path joins conditions with AND.
  • A leaf’s class fraction estimates high-demand probability here; it is not automatically calibrated.
  • Trees can learn interactions without explicitly creating interaction columns.
  • A small tree is inspectable; many leaves make the full model difficult to read.

Trace a path from root to leaf. What information does the model ignore on that path?

How the tree chooses a split

For the root’s selected feature, compare candidate thresholds using weighted child Gini impurity.

The learner favors purer child groups, subject to constraints such as minimum leaf size. This plot inspects the chosen feature; training also compared the other features.

Local explanations for a fixed forest

The forest is already trained. We explain its high-demand probability for one fixed validation hour.

  • LIME fits a simple surrogate near this observation.
  • SHAP allocates the prediction minus a reference output among features.

The explanation method does not retrain the forest. A sensible explanation can still describe an incorrect prediction.

LIME: a local surrogate

  1. Generate perturbed tabular rows; query the forest on them.
  2. Weight rows by proximity to the observation.
  3. Fit a small linear model to approximate the forest’s outputs.

\[g_x=\arg\min_{g\in G}L(f,g,\pi_x)+\Omega(g)\]

\(L\) penalizes local disagreement; \(\Omega\) limits explanation complexity.

The surrogate approximates model predictions, not observed labels (Ribeiro et al. 2016).

3–6 min. Source: https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/lime.html . The sparse surrogate trades fidelity for simplicity; the predictive forest is fixed.

A neighborhood for tabular data

What counts as “near” this hour?

  • Mark hour and binary inputs as categorical for perturbation.
  • Scale continuous inputs internally when calculating proximity.
  • Choose a kernel width, sample budget, seed, and number of explanation terms.
  • Individual feature sampling can create implausible combinations of hour, weather, and working-day status.

A different neighborhood can lead to a different explanation.

6–8 min. Source: Molnar LIME and https://lime-ml.readthedocs.io/en/latest/lime.html . Here hour is treated categorically by the explainer although the forest splits it numerically. This makes the explanation encoding explicit.

Does the surrogate fit the forest?

Inspect both diagnostics:

  • Point discrepancy: surrogate prediction versus forest probability at this hour.
  • Weighted \(R^2\): agreement on LIME’s sampled neighborhood.

These are surrogate diagnostics, not held-out classification accuracy. A small point discrepancy can coexist with poor neighborhood fit.

LIME stability

Repeat the explanation of the same model and hour with different random seeds.

  • Do the strongest conditions and their signs change?
  • Do local fit and point discrepancy change?
  • Would the explanation support the same operator decision?

Seed stability tests sampling variability only; it does not test model retraining or population shift.

SHAP: allocate a prediction difference

\[f(x)=\phi_0+\sum_j\phi_j(x)\]

  • Feature values act as players; contributions average marginal changes over coalition contexts.
  • \(\phi_0\) is the reference model output.
  • Here we explain high-demand probability, so contributions are in probability units.
  • TreeSHAP computes attributions for this tree ensemble (Lundberg and Lee 2017; Lundberg et al. 2020).

The chosen background and dependence assumptions define the attribution question.

14–17 min. Source: https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/shap.html . Distinguish the additive attribution representation from LIME’s neighborhood regression.

What does an absent feature mean?

For this interventional explanation, combine observed features with background rows:

\[v_x(S)=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{z\in B}f(x_S,z_{\bar S})\]

  • Features in \(S\) take this hour’s values; remaining values come from a background row.
  • \(v_x(\varnothing)\) is the mean model output on the background, not the observed positive rate.
  • Absent does not mean zero. Correlated inputs can produce unrealistic mixed rows.

17–19 min. Source: https://shap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/shap.TreeExplainer.html . The word interventional is an algorithmic setting, not evidence that this observational model identifies causal effects.

We can now produce explanations. How should we judge them?

Property Workshop check What remains untested?
Local fidelity LIME neighborhood fit and point discrepancy Behavior on a different neighborhood
Attribution consistency SHAP reconstruction Causality and usefulness
Stability Repeat seeds or change background Retraining and future populations
Human usefulness Operator can read and act appropriately Requires evaluation with users

No single scalar certifies interpretability.

Theory 3, 0–3 min. Workshop evaluation rubric. Compare with Molnar evaluation chapter: https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/evaluation.html .

Local versus aggregated explanations

One explanation describes one hour. Summaries describe the set of explained hours.

Mean absolute SHAP values show how much each feature moves probabilities across that set.

  • They lose the sign of contributions.
  • They depend on which hours are explained and which background is used.
  • They measure attribution magnitude, not the drop in prediction quality after removing a feature.

3–5 min. Source: https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/shap.html . Use a small fixed sample of validation hours, not the test period.

Dependence and unrealistic combinations

Imagine holding hour and working-day status fixed while borrowing weather from unrelated hours.

  • The resulting row may be uncommon or implausible.
  • A predictor can behave unexpectedly away from its training distribution.
  • Correlated or redundant features can share attributions differently under different value functions.

Question: would adding apparent temperature make the explanation more informative, or mostly add a correlated measurement?

5–7 min. This is why atemp is omitted in the small example. Interventional and conditional attribution questions differ; do not assume either answers a causal question.

Is this another multi-objective problem?

Often yes: predictive error, explanation complexity, stability, and explanation time can compete.

For candidate decision trees, consider:

\[\min_h\left(1-\mathrm{BA}_{valid}(h),\;\#\mathrm{leaves}(h)\right)\]

Leaf count is a structural proxy for readability, not a universal interpretability score.

A performance–interpretability tradeoff is possible; it is not inevitable.

Turn preferences into a selection rule

For the exercise, set a tolerance of 0.02 absolute balanced accuracy below the best candidate.

  1. Keep trees within this tolerance on validation data.
  2. Choose the feasible tree with the fewest leaves.
  3. Break a leaf-count tie using lower validation error.
  4. Evaluate the chosen model once on the untouched test period.

The tolerance is a stakeholder preference for this exercise, not a universal rule.

19–21 min. Constraints are an alternative to assigning a weighted sum. Test results must not be used to revise this selection.

Explanation complexity is another choice

For a fixed forest and observation, change LIME’s term budget.

  • Two terms may be easier to read but omit model behavior.
  • Six terms may fit better but demand more attention.
  • Measure fit and elapsed time; a larger budget does not guarantee a better explanation for a person.

This is a different optimization problem from choosing a predictive model.

21–24 min, including results. LIME optimizes a local surrogate under a specified feature budget. Measured elapsed times are machine-dependent and not an algorithmic benchmark.

What the objectives leave out

Leaf count and local fit do not measure everything an operator needs.

  • Are the feature names and units understandable?
  • Are the reference population and output class clear?
  • Does the explanation remain useful when the prediction is wrong?
  • Does it support the intended decision without encouraging causal overinterpretation?

Test comprehension with the intended audience. A Pareto frontier does not decide what is acceptable.

24–27 min. Use an operator role-play: ask a student to interpret one rule or attribution. Interpretability depends on task, user, and representation.
