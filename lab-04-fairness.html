1. Fairness Failures in the Real World

For each case, ask: what was the system trying to do, who was affected, and what should have been checked before deployment?

From Headlines to a Fairness Audit

These cases motivate the questions we will use throughout the lab:

  • Data and proxies: who appears in the dataset, and which features encode group membership?
  • Targets: does the observed label measure the outcome we actually want?
  • Evaluation: which groups and intersections need separate metrics, and which errors matter most?
  • Interventions: does a proposed fix improve the intended behavior across realistic contexts?
  • Deployment: how do people use the output, and who bears the consequences?

We now formalize these questions before auditing and mitigating disparities in the Adult and diabetes datasets.

Prediction Setup

We frame supervised prediction with random variables:

  • \(X\): observed input features, such as pixels, SAT score, income attributes, hospital admission data, or ZIP code.
  • \(A\): sensitive or protected group attribute, such as race, sex, age group, or disability status.
  • \(Y\): true or observed label, such as college success, loan repayment, recidivism, income, or readmission.
  • \(\hat{Y}= h(X)\): the prediction produced by a learned model.

The full population distribution P(X, A, Y) is not available.

  • We only have samples.
  • Those samples may be missing, noisy, unlabeled, unrepresentative, or historically biased.
  • This is why fairness belongs in data-centric AI: the dataset is part of the model’s behavior.

What Bias Is Not

  • Bias is not necessarily malicious. It can arise even when everyone involved has good intentions.

  • Bias is not one and done. A model may become unfair as populations, pipelines, policies, or deployment settings change.

  • Bias is not new. Researchers and practitioners have discussed discrimination, fairness, and measurement harms for decades.

  • Fairness work belongs throughout the dataset lifecycle:

    • collection, annotation, and preprocessing,
    • validation, monitoring, and model use.

Why Different Treatment Happens

Different treatment usually does not require a programmer to intentionally encode racism, sexism, or another discriminatory rule.

Common data causes include:

  • the training set is not representative of the deployment population,
  • absence of data reflects historical exclusion,
  • small subgroup sample sizes do not generalize well,
  • labels encode previous biased decisions,
  • features include proxies for protected attributes,
  • and data quality differs across groups.

So an audit should ask not only “what did the model learn?” but also “what world did the dataset measure?”

What Bias Is

A useful working definition:

A machine-learning system is biased when its data, modeling process, or deployment context creates systematically different errors, opportunities, or harms for different groups.

  • Data collection bias: who appears in the data, who is missing, and under what conditions measurements are made.
  • Algorithmic process bias: how features, objectives, thresholds, and validation metrics encode priorities.
  • Deployment bias: how predictions are interpreted and used by humans or institutions.

Quantitative Fairness Definitions

Use these variables:

  • Y: true label; Y_hat: model prediction; S: risk score.
  • X: ordinary features; A: sensitive attribute, such as sex, race, or age group.

Statistical Parity and Equal Opportunity

Statistical parity asks for equal positive prediction rates:

  • P(Y_hat = 1 | A = a) = P(Y_hat = 1 | A = b)
  • Usually operationalized with selection rate and demographic parity difference.

Equal opportunity asks for equal true positive rates:

  • P(Y_hat = 1 | Y = 1, A = a) = P(Y_hat = 1 | Y = 1, A = b)
  • Useful when missing qualified or truly positive cases is the central harm.

College-Admissions Toy Example

A simplified admissions task makes the issue concrete:

  • X is SAT score (a standardized test widely used for college admissions in the United States).
  • A is group membership, shown as two shapes.
  • Y is whether an applicant would succeed in college.
  • Y_hat is the admit/reject decision.

A model can look accurate overall while one group lives in a different part of the feature space.

One group has a shifted score distribution because of external conditions such as test preparation access or retake opportunities.

  • Even a “shape-blind” model may infer group membership from correlated features. Omitting A does not remove its influence.

Incompatibility and Trade-Offs

Fairness definitions can be mutually incompatible.

  • Calibration and error-rate balance generally cannot both hold when groups have different base rates, except in special cases.

That is why this lab does not ask whether a model is simply “fair” or “unfair”. Instead, we ask:

  • What decision is being made?
  • Which group attribute is relevant to audit?
  • Which errors are most harmful?
  • Is the target label a good proxy for the real-world construct?
  • Which fairness metric matches the intended use?
  • What data changes would reduce the problem before changing the model?

No Fair Lunch

The incompatibility result is often phrased as a no fair lunch problem. In a real-world ML system, we may want all of the following:

  • statistical parity,
  • equal false negative rates,
  • equal false positive rates,
  • high accuracy across subgroups.

In general, these cannot all be achieved simultaneously unless the groups have the same underlying distribution.

  • This is rare because data collection, access, measurement, and historical decisions already differ across groups.

Fairness as a Multi-Objective Problem

A data-centric fairness workflow is naturally a multi-objective optimization problem. We rarely optimize only predictive performance.

For a model or decision rule h, we may want to:

  • maximize utility: balanced_accuracy(h), profit, recall, or another task metric,
  • minimize disparity: demographic parity gap, equal opportunity gap, equalized odds gap,
  • minimize data risk: missingness, subgroup undercoverage, label noise, proxy leakage,
  • satisfy constraints: minimum subgroup sample size, maximum false negative gap, acceptable documentation quality.

One compact formulation is maximize (utility(h), -fairness_gap(h), -data_risk(h))

  • Subject to domain constraints such as FNR_gap(h) <= delta and support(group) >= n_min.

There are non-dominated choices: one model is better on accuracy, another is better on fairness, another may be easier to interpret.

Ways to Choose Among Objectives

Strategy What it does Risk
Hard constraint maximize accuracy subject to a fairness limit the limit may be arbitrary
Scalarization combine objectives, e.g. score = accuracy - lambda * disparity hides value choices inside weights
Lexicographic rule satisfy fairness first, then optimize accuracy can discard useful trade-offs
Pareto frontier keep all non-dominated candidates for review still requires a human decision

What Is [Fairlearn]](https://fairlearn.org/)?

Fairlearn is an open-source Python toolkit for assessing and mitigating fairness issues in machine learning systems.

  • Assess disparities: MetricFrame compares performance across groups and their intersections.
    • Fairness metrics summarize gaps in selection or error rates.
  • Mitigate disparities: ExponentiatedGradient adds fairness constraints during training; ThresholdOptimizer adjusts decisions after training.
  • Use familiar models: integrate fairness assessment and mitigation with scikit-learn estimators and tabular workflows.

In this lab: train a baseline → audit groups → apply an intervention → compare accuracy and fairness.

Interpretation

If the model predicts positive outcomes for one group much more often than another, demographic parity is violated.

  • If false positive or false negative rates differ strongly, error-rate balance is violated.

A data-centric audit asks whether the observed gap comes from:

  • different base rates in the collected data,
  • missing or low-quality measurements for one group,
  • proxy variables that reconstruct the sensitive attribute,
  • an objective function that optimizes average accuracy while hiding subgroup errors,
  • or a threshold that is not appropriate for all groups.

6. Fairness Interventions: Where Do We Act?

The stage tells us where we intervene; the fairness definition tells us what we try to achieve.

Stage What changes? Examples
Pre-processing Training data or its representation data augmentation, reweighting, feature transformations
In-processing The learning procedure fairness constraints, penalties, adversarial training
Post-processing Decisions from an already trained model group-specific thresholds, randomized decision rules

Pipeline: data → pre-processing → training with in-processing → scores → post-processing → decisions.

Pre-Processing: Change the Data Before Training

  • Adjust training examples, weights, labels, or features to address a documented disparity.
  • Data augmentation: reuse the techniques from the data augmentation lab.
    • Target underrepresented groups or group-label combinations.
  • Other examples: reweight group-label combinations or transform features to reduce sensitive-information leakage.

In this lab: removing a suspected proxy and retraining is a pre-processing experiment.

  • Removing sex and race alone is fairness through unawareness; correlated features can preserve disparity.
  • Limit: changing the data does not guarantee the selected fairness criterion will hold.

In-Processing: Change How the Model Learns

  • Optimize predictive performance subject to a fairness constraint, or add a disparity penalty to the objective.
    • Examples: constrained optimization, adversarial debiasing, and reduction-based learning.
  • Requires access to training; group constraints generally require sensitive attributes during fitting.

In this lab: ExponentiatedGradient uses an EqualizedOdds() constraint.

  • It repeatedly retrains a base estimator with adapted example weights to seek a constrained solution.
  • This is in-processing: weights change inside the fairness-constrained learning procedure.W
    • They are not fixed once before training.

Limit: improving the chosen constraint can reduce utility or leave other disparities unresolved.

Post-Processing: Change Decisions After Training

  • Keep the fitted predictive model fixed and adjust how its scores become decisions.
    • Examples: group-specific thresholds and randomized decisions chosen for a fairness constraint.
  • Requires validation or calibration data; fitting a decision rule is still a learning step. Keep the final test set separate.

In this lab: ThresholdOptimizer with prefit=True adjusts decisions for demographic parity.

  • Its group-specific rule needs sensitive_features when predicting.
  • A single global threshold sweep also acts after training, but does not automatically enforce a fairness criterion.

Limit: decision changes cannot repair missing population coverage or an invalid target label.

Source for the methods: Fairlearn mitigation guide.

Mitigation A: Post-Processing with ThresholdOptimizer

ThresholdOptimizer changes decision thresholds by sensitive group after a model is trained.

  • This is useful when scores are available and the deployment decision is threshold-based.
  • Here we enforce demographic parity.
    • That means selection rates are pushed closer together. Whether this is desirable depends on the application.
Code 
threshold_dp = ThresholdOptimizer(estimator=baseline, constraints="demographic_parity", objective="balanced_accuracy_score", predict_method="predict_proba", prefit=True,)

y_pred_threshold_dp = threshold_dp.predict(X_test, sensitive_features=sex_test)

7. Data-Centric Remediation Ideas

The proxy-removal exercise is a pre-processing intervention: change features, then retrain.

  • Collecting better data or revisiting the target goes beyond algorithmic mitigation.

Try at least two of these interventions:

  • Revisit the target: predict a less historically loaded target, or separate qualification from observed outcome.
  • Improve representation: collect more records or use data augmentation for underrepresented groups; report subgroup uncertainty.
  • Improve measurement: reduce missingness and standardize ambiguous categories.
  • Remove or transform proxy features after documenting what utility is lost.
  • Use stratified validation: require acceptable performance on each important subgroup before release.
  • Monitor after deployment: repeat the audit when data distributions shift.

Data Heterogeneity and Proxy Targets

Two data-centric warnings matter for the second case study:

  • Data heterogeneity: groups can differ in feature distributions, measurement quality, and clinical histories.

    • Language used in records may also differ. An average model can hide these patterns.

  • Proxy targets: an objective-looking label may measure the wrong thing.

    • Predicting healthcare cost may reflect unequal access and spending rather than illness.

  • What does readmitted_30_days measure?

    • Patient need, hospital behavior, patient access, or a mixture of all three?

Healthcare Interpretation

For readmission prediction:

  • A false negative may miss a patient who needs follow-up.
  • A false positive may cause unnecessary outreach, cost, or monitoring.

Data-centric questions:

  • Is readmission within 30 days a proxy for patient need, hospital access, insurance, or care quality?
  • Are some groups less likely to return to the same hospital even when they are still sick?
  • Are diagnostic codes and procedures measured consistently across hospitals and groups?
  • Does the dataset encode treatment history, or only outcomes after unequal treatment has already occurred?
