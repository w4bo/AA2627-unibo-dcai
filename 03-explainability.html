Why explain a model?

A model that is accurate on a test set can still be right for the wrong reasons.

  • Debugging: is the model using leaked, spurious, or proxy information?
  • Trust and adoption: can a domain expert check the reasoning?
  • Accountability: can an affected person understand and contest a decision?
  • Science: what has the model learned about the data-generating process?

In data-centric AI the most useful question is often not “why did the model predict this?” but “what in the data made the model behave this way, and what should we fix?”

Interpretability and explainability

The terms are used loosely; a workable distinction (doshivelez2017towards?; lipton2018mythos?):

Term Meaning
Interpretability A human can understand how the model maps inputs to outputs (by design)
Explainability We produce an explanation of a (possibly opaque) model after training
Explanation An answer to a why-question, for a given audience and purpose

Explanations are social: people prefer contrastive (“why P rather than Q?”), selective (few causes), and plausible answers (miller2019explanation?).

There is no explanation “in general”: always ask who needs it and for what decision.

Regulation asks for explanations

  • GDPR (art. 13-15, 22, recital 71): information about the “logic involved” in automated decisions. Whether this amounts to a right to explanation is debated (goodman2017european?; wachter2017right?).
  • EU AI Act (euaiact2024?):
    • art. 13: high-risk systems must be transparent enough for deployers to interpret their output;
    • art. 86: a person affected by a decision based on a high-risk system has a right to an explanation of the role of the system in the decision.

Legal duties are about understandable, actionable information, not about a specific algorithm such as SHAP.

Axis Options
When intrinsic (interpretable by design) vs post-hoc (explain a trained model)
Scope global (the whole model) vs local (one prediction)
Access model-specific (uses internals) vs model-agnostic (only predict)
Output feature attributions, rules, examples, counterfactuals, training-data attributions

The rest of the lecture follows this map, from interpretable models to Shapley values, and then from features to data.

Interpretable models

Some models are explanations of themselves (molnar2022interpretable?):

  • Linear / logistic regression: \(f(x) = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j\); coefficients are effects holding other features fixed (scale-dependent, distorted by correlation).
  • Decision trees and rule lists: a prediction is a readable path of conditions (only if shallow).
  • Generalized additive models (GAMs): \(g(E[y]) = \beta_0 + \sum_j s_j(x_j)\), with shape functions \(s_j\) that can be plotted (lou2012intelligible?); Explainable Boosting Machines learn them with boosting (nori2019interpretml?).

For high-stakes decisions, (rudin2019stop?) argues we should prefer interpretable models over explaining black boxes, because post-hoc explanations can be unfaithful. On tabular data, the accuracy gap is often small.

Global post-hoc: permutation importance

Shuffle feature \(j\) in the evaluation data, breaking its link with the target, and measure the performance drop (Breiman 2001; fisher2019all?):

\[ PI_j = \text{score}(f, X, y) - \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R} \text{score}(f, X^{(\pi_r, j)}, y) \]

  • Model-agnostic, cheap, measured in units of the metric.
  • Computed on held-out data it measures what the model needs to generalize; on training data it can reward overfitting.
  • With correlated features, shuffling creates unrealistic rows, and importance is split or hidden between correlated features.

Global post-hoc: partial dependence, ICE, ALE

Partial dependence (PDP) shows the average prediction as a function of one feature (friedman2001greedy?):

\[ PD_j(z) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(z, x_{i,-j}) \]

  • ICE curves plot one line per instance instead of the average, revealing heterogeneous effects and interactions (goldstein2015peeking?).
  • ALE plots average local changes within intervals of \(x_j\), avoiding unrealistic combinations when features are correlated (apley2020visualizing?).

A flat PDP does not imply “no effect”: opposite effects in subgroups can cancel out. Always look at ICE curves too.

Local post-hoc: LIME

LIME explains one prediction with a simple surrogate \(g\) (e.g., sparse linear) fitted on perturbed samples around \(x\) (ribeiro2016should?):

\[ \xi(x) = \mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{g \in G} \; \mathcal{L}(f, g, \pi_x) + \Omega(g) \]

  • \(\pi_x\) weights samples by proximity to \(x\); \(\Omega(g)\) penalizes complexity (number of features).
  • Intuitive and model-agnostic, but the result depends on the perturbation distribution, kernel width, and random seed.

The famous husky vs wolf example: the classifier relied on snow in the background, a property of the data collection, not of the animals (ribeiro2016should?).

From surrogates to a principled attribution

We want to split a prediction among features:

\[ f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{M} \phi_j \]

Which split is “fair”? Cooperative game theory answered this question in 1953 (shapley1953value?):

  • players cooperate in a coalition and produce a payout;
  • the Shapley value divides the payout according to each player’s average marginal contribution.

SHAP maps features to players and the prediction to the payout (lundberg2017unified?).

Cooperative games

A cooperative game is a set of players \(N = \{1, \dots, M\}\) and a value function \(v: 2^N \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) with \(v(\varnothing) = 0\) or a baseline.

  • \(v(S)\) is the payout that coalition \(S\) obtains on its own.
  • The marginal contribution of player \(j\) to coalition \(S\) (with \(j \notin S\)) is \(v(S \cup \{j\}) - v(S)\).

The marginal contribution depends on who is already in the coalition: two features may be useful only together (interaction), or redundant (each useful alone, not both).

The Shapley value

The Shapley value of player \(j\) averages its marginal contribution over all coalitions of the other players:

\[ \phi_j(v) = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(M-|S|-1)!}{M!} \big[v(S \cup \{j\}) - v(S)\big] \]

Equivalent permutation view: order the players at random, add them one by one, and record what \(j\) adds when it joins:

\[ \phi_j(v) = \frac{1}{M!} \sum_{\sigma \in \Pi(N)} \big[v(\text{Pre}_j(\sigma) \cup \{j\}) - v(\text{Pre}_j(\sigma))\big] \]

The weight \(\frac{|S|!(M-|S|-1)!}{M!}\) is exactly the fraction of orderings in which the players before \(j\) are \(S\).

The Shapley axioms

The Shapley value is the unique attribution satisfying (shapley1953value?):

Axiom Meaning for features
Efficiency \(\sum_j \phi_j = v(N) - v(\varnothing)\): contributions add up to prediction minus baseline
Symmetry two features that contribute equally to every coalition get the same value
Dummy (null player) a feature that never changes \(v\) gets \(\phi_j = 0\)
Additivity for \(v = v_1 + v_2\) (e.g., an ensemble), \(\phi_j(v) = \phi_j(v_1) + \phi_j(v_2)\)

Uniqueness is the key argument for Shapley-based attributions: if you accept the axioms, there is no other choice.

Worked example: three features

A flight with bad weather (W), late departure (L), and a holiday (H). Baseline \(v(\varnothing) = 0.30\), prediction \(v(\{W,L,H\}) = 0.76\):

Worked example: the Shapley values

Each feature joins in \(3! = 6\) orders; we average its marginal contributions:

Feature \(\phi_j\) (subset formula) \(\phi_j\) (average over orders)

Efficiency holds: \(0.30 + \sum_j \phi_j = 0.76\). Weather gets the largest share, partly because it interacts with late departure (\(v(W,L) - v(L) > v(W) - v(\varnothing)\)).

From games to models: what is \(v(S)\)?

For a model \(f\) and an instance \(x\), SHAP defines the value of a set of “known” features \(S\) as the expected prediction when the other features are unknown (lundberg2017unified?):

\[ v_x(S) = E\big[f(X) \mid X_S = x_S\big] \quad \text{(observational / conditional)} \]

\[ v_x(S) = E_{X_{\bar S}}\big[f(x_S, X_{\bar S})\big] \quad \text{(interventional / marginal)} \]

  • \(v_x(\varnothing) = E[f(X)]\) is the baseline, computed over a background (reference) dataset.
  • \(v_x(N) = f(x)\) is the prediction, so by efficiency \(f(x) = E[f(X)] + \sum_j \phi_j\).

“Unknown” never means “set to zero”: unknown features are averaged over the background data.

Conditional or interventional?

Interventional (marginal) Observational (conditional)

Correlated features may evaluate unrealistic rows credit spreads to correlated features, even unused ones

The choice is “true to the model or true to the data” (chen2020true?); (janzing2020feature?) argue the interventional version is the right one to describe the model’s mechanism.

SHAP: additive feature attribution

(lundberg2017unified?) define a class of explanations \(g(z') = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_j z'_j\) with \(z' \in \{0,1\}^M\) (feature present or absent), and show that only Shapley values satisfy:

  • Local accuracy: \(f(x) = g(x') = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_j\);
  • Missingness: absent features get \(\phi_j = 0\);
  • Consistency: if a model changes so that a feature’s marginal contribution never decreases, its attribution does not decrease.

LIME, DeepLIFT, and Layer-wise Relevance Propagation also fit this additive form, but they violate some of these properties.

Computing SHAP values: the cost problem

Exact Shapley values need \(v(S)\) for every subset: \(2^M\) coalitions, each an expectation over the background data.

Features \(M\) Coalitions \(2^M\)
10 1,024
20 1,048,576
40 \(\approx 10^{12}\)

Three families of solutions:

  1. Sampling: estimate the permutation average with random orderings (strumbelj2014explaining?).
  2. KernelSHAP: solve a weighted regression that recovers Shapley values (lundberg2017unified?).
  3. Model-specific exact algorithms: TreeSHAP, LinearSHAP (lundberg2020local?).

Permutation sampling

Monte Carlo estimate of the permutation formula (strumbelj2014explaining?):

  1. Draw a random ordering \(\sigma\) and a background row \(z\).
  2. Build two hybrid rows: features before \(j\) in \(\sigma\) (and \(j\) itself) from \(x\), the rest from \(z\), with and without \(j\).
  3. Record the difference in predictions; average over \(K\) samples.
  • Unbiased; error decreases as \(1/\sqrt{K}\).
  • Each sample needs only two model calls: model-agnostic and parallel.
  • The shap PermutationExplainer walks each sampled ordering forward and backward, so the estimated values always add up exactly to \(f(x) - E[f(X)]\).

KernelSHAP

Sample coalitions \(z' \in \{0,1\}^M\), evaluate \(v_x(z')\) by replacing absent features with background values, and fit a weighted linear model:

\[ \min_{\phi} \sum_{z'} \pi_x(z') \Big[v_x(z') - \phi_0 - \sum_j \phi_j z'_j\Big]^2, \qquad \pi_x(z') = \frac{M-1}{\binom{M}{|z'|}\,|z'|\,(M-|z'|)} \]

With this Shapley kernel, the regression solution equals the Shapley values (lundberg2017unified?). Coalitions that are almost empty or almost full get the largest weight, because they isolate individual effects:

\(|z'|\) (features present, \(M=6\)) 1 2 3 4 5
kernel weight \(\pi\) 0.167 0.042 0.028 0.042 0.167

TreeSHAP and LinearSHAP

TreeSHAP computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in polynomial time, \(O(T L D^2)\) for \(T\) trees with \(L\) leaves and depth \(D\), by pushing all subsets through the tree simultaneously (lundberg2020local?).

  • path-dependent mode uses the training counts stored in the tree nodes (no background data needed);
  • interventional mode uses an explicit background dataset.

LinearSHAP: for \(f(x) = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j\) with independent features,

\[ \phi_j = \beta_j \big(x_j - E[X_j]\big) \]

Both keep the trained model fixed: SHAP never retrains a model per feature subset.

From local to global explanations

SHAP gives a matrix \(\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times M}\): one row per explained instance, one column per feature.

View Computed as Answers
Local (waterfall / force plot) one row \(\phi_{i\cdot}\) why this prediction differs from the baseline
Global importance \(I_j = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \lvert\phi_{ij}\rvert\) which features the model uses most
Summary (beeswarm) all \(\phi_{ij}\) coloured by \(x_{ij}\) direction and spread of effects
Dependence plot \(\phi_{ij}\) vs \(x_{ij}\) shape of the effect, interactions
Interaction values \(\phi_{i,jk}\) pairwise effects beyond the sum (lundberg2020local?)

Global SHAP importance is built bottom-up from consistent local explanations, unlike permutation importance, which measures loss of accuracy.

Pitfalls of feature attributions

  • Not causal: SHAP explains the model, not the world. A large \(\phi_j\) does not mean that changing \(x_j\) changes the outcome.
  • Background matters: a different reference population gives a different baseline and different values.
  • Correlated features: unrealistic hybrid rows (interventional) or credit given to unused features (conditional).
  • Can be fooled: a model can behave discriminatorily on real data while looking innocent to perturbation-based explainers (slack2020fooling?).
  • Disagreement: LIME, SHAP, and gradients often disagree on the top features of the same prediction (krishna2022disagreement?).
  • Sanity checks: an explanation that does not change when the model is randomized explains nothing about the model (adebayo2018sanity?).

Counterfactual explanations

A counterfactual answers “what is the smallest change to \(x\) that changes the decision?” (wachter2017counterfactual?):

\[ x' = \mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{x'} \; \lambda \big(f(x') - y'\big)^2 + d(x, x') \]

  • Contrastive and actionable: “with 6 more hours of work per week, the prediction would be > 50K”.
  • Must be plausible (close to the data manifold) and actionable (do not ask people to change age or sex).
  • Several diverse counterfactuals give a fuller picture (mothilal2020explaining?).

Data-centric warning: a counterfactual that relies on a proxy (e.g., marital status for sex) reveals a data problem, not a useful recourse.

The data-centric turn

So far: explain predictions in terms of features. Data-centric AI adds two questions:

  1. Explanations as data-debugging tools: what do attributions reveal about leakage, shortcuts, proxies, and label errors?
  2. Explanations in terms of data: which training examples are responsible for a prediction, and which examples help or hurt the model?

The same Shapley machinery works for both: players can be features (SHAP) or training points (Data Shapley).

Debugging data with attributions: leakage

Leakage is information in the training data that will not be available, or will not mean the same thing, at prediction time (kaufman2012leakage?).

Typical symptoms in explanations:

  • one feature dominates global importance far more than domain knowledge suggests;
  • the feature is recorded after the outcome (e.g., “number of follow-up visits” when predicting readmission);
  • validation accuracy is suspiciously high and collapses after deployment.

Fix the data, not the model: remove or re-time the feature, and rebuild the split so that information flows only from past to future.

Debugging data: shortcuts and spurious correlations

Shortcut learning: the model solves the benchmark with a decision rule that does not transfer to the intended task (geirhos2020shortcut?).

Domain Shortcut in the data
Image classification background (snow, grass), watermarks
Medical imaging hospital-specific tokens or scanner artifacts
Tabular data data-entry codes, form versions, IDs correlated with the label

Attributions reveal shortcuts when an irrelevant feature receives large, consistent contributions. The fix is data-centric: collect counter-examples, rebalance, or remove the artifact.

Debugging data: proxies and label errors

Proxies: removing a sensitive attribute is not enough if other features encode it (zip code, relationship status). Attributions and dependence plots show where the information flows (see the fairness lab).

Label errors: real datasets contain mislabeled examples (northcutt2021confident?).

  • Confident learning compares out-of-sample predicted probabilities with the given labels and flags examples whose label is unlikely.
  • Local explanations help decide whether a disagreement is a model error (strange attributions) or a label error (sensible attributions, wrong label).

Explaining with examples

Humans often explain with cases: “this loan resembles these approved ones”.

  • Prototypes and criticisms: representative examples of the data, and examples poorly represented by the prototypes (kim2016examples?).
  • Nearest neighbours in the model’s representation space.
  • Counterfactual examples (previous slides).

Criticisms are a data-quality tool: they surface rare subgroups, outliers, and possibly mislabeled rows.

Training-data attribution

Which training points are responsible for a prediction?

  • Leave-one-out: retrain without point \(i\) and measure the change. Exact but expensive.
  • Influence functions approximate leave-one-out with a first-order expansion around the optimum (koh2017understanding?):

\[ \mathcal{I}(z_i, z_{test}) = -\nabla_\theta L(z_{test}, \hat\theta)^\top H_{\hat\theta}^{-1} \nabla_\theta L(z_i, \hat\theta) \]

  • TracIn sums gradient dot-products over training checkpoints (pruthi2020estimating?).
  • Datamodels learn a (linear) map from training subsets to predictions (ilyas2022datamodels?).

Uses: find mislabeled or harmful training examples, explain a prediction by its “supporting” data.

Data Shapley: valuing training data

Treat training points as players and model performance as the payout (ghorbani2019data?):

\[ \phi_i = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{S \subseteq D \setminus \{i\}} \binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1} \big[U(S \cup \{i\}) - U(S)\big] \]

where \(U(S)\) is the validation performance of a model trained on \(S\).

SHAP Data Shapley
Players features of one instance training examples
Value function expected prediction of a fixed model performance of a model retrained on a subset
Use explain a prediction find harmful data, prioritize cleaning and collection

Computing data values

Exact Data Shapley requires retraining on exponentially many subsets. Practical approaches:

  • Truncated Monte Carlo: sample permutations of the training set and stop when adding points no longer changes performance (ghorbani2019data?).
  • KNN-Shapley: for a \(K\)-nearest-neighbour utility, exact values follow from a recursion over training points sorted by distance, in \(O(n \log n)\) per test point (jia2019efficient?).

Low or negative values point to noisy labels, outliers, or out-of-distribution data. Removing the lowest-valued points should hurt performance less than removing random ones, or even improve it.

Documentation is explainability for data

Explanations of a model are incomplete without explanations of its data:

  • Datasheets for datasets: motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, uses, distribution, maintenance (gebru2021datasheets?).
  • Model cards: intended use, evaluation data, metrics disaggregated by group, limitations (mitchell2019model?).

These documents answer questions no attribution method can: who collected the data, why, and what it should not be used for.

A data-centric explainability workflow

  1. Before training: document the data (datasheet), check provenance and timing of every feature.
  2. After training: global attributions (permutation importance, SHAP) and ICE/ALE plots; compare with domain knowledge.
  3. Suspicious feature? Check for leakage, shortcuts, proxies; fix the data, retrain, and compare.
  4. Suspicious examples? Confident learning, data valuation, influence; relabel, remove, or collect more data.
  5. Per decision: local SHAP and counterfactuals for the affected person, avoiding proxy-based recourse.
  6. Report: model card with disaggregated metrics and the limitations of the explanations themselves.

Summary

  • Interpretable models first, post-hoc explanations when needed, and never one explanation for every audience.
  • Shapley values are the unique fair attribution under four axioms; SHAP applies them to features with a background-based value function.
  • Exact computation is exponential: sampling, KernelSHAP, and TreeSHAP make it practical.
  • Global SHAP importance is the average magnitude of local explanations.
  • Explanations explain models, not the world; check them against domain knowledge and sanity tests.
  • In data-centric AI, explanations are a diagnostic for the data: leakage, shortcuts, proxies, label errors, and data value.
